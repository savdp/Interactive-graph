Hesiod stated it clearly in his work Works and Days (c. 700 BCE): "Do not put your work off till tomorrow and the day after; for a sluggish worker does not fill his barn, nor one who puts off his work". But is it so easy?

A vicious cycle

Starting from a stage of not really thinking of the task to be completed, work piles up as the days pass. Still, the procrastinator keeps putting off the work until they end up cursing themselves and neglecting their other responsibilities. Eventually, they end up working fast to reach the looming deadline, while making sacrifices to get their work done. Task aversion leads to decreased productivity and avoidance coping, with procrastinators internalizing these feelings of failure, anxiety and decision fatigue, which might even lead to confidence issues, low self-esteem and depression.

Time or emotion management?

Procrastination is not simply a matter of poor time management, but rather a complex issue rooted in emotional and motivational factors. A poor concept of time may exacerbate the problem, but an inability to manage emotions seems to be its very foundation.

"Just do it"

Joseph Ferrari, Fellow of the Association for Psychological Science and Professor of psychology at DePaul University, notes that procrastination has nothing to do with time-management. "As I tell people, to tell the chronic procrastinator to just do it would be like saying to a clinically depressed person, cheer up", he says.

Breaking the procrastination cycle is not easy. In a perfectionist world, anyone not delivering is considered as failing. However, there is hope: An 18-year-long study published recently in the Journal of Personality & Social Psychology found that people tend to procrastinate less as they move through young adulthood.

Tips and tricks

If you find yourself delaying to complete tasks, you might consider lowering the bar to start with: To quote Sheryl Sandberg, the former COO of Facebook, from her book Lean In, "done is better than perfect". Then, you might break the task into smaller sub-tasks and/or assign a time limit: Committing to work on something for as little as 10 minutes might help you regulate your emotions and motivate you to continue working on your goal, while eventually cultivating emotional resilience. This idea draws back to the Pomodoro Technique introduced in the 1990s by Italian entrepreneur Francesco Cirillo; nowadays, online tools such as Pomofocus make it even easier to break work into 25-minute focused intervals before taking short breaks.

Mel Robbins, author of the 5 Second Rule book, suggests to count down from 5 to 1 and immediately physically move and start the task before your brain can make up excuses. In her view, the 5-4-3-2-1 method is a simple mental trick that works when one feels the urge to delay or overthink a task. Finally, you might consider the Eisenhower Matrix and focus on executing the urgent and important tasks first, before proceeding to the non-urgent and not important ones at a later stage.

Willpower is not enough

In either case, procrastinators need to treat themselves with kindness. From professional pressure, financial challenges and interpersonal problems to digital notifications, information overload and social media comparison, modern society is full of distractions. Navigating the world requires effort to reclaim focus: As Stanford Medicine experts explain, the human brain gets a boost in the form of a dopamine hit when something fresh comes up, and it takes a lot of energy to perform the effortful, long-term thinking that demands focus and concentration.

Rebecca Roache, Associate Professor in philosophy at the University of London, even suggests to stop thinking about procrastination as the opposite of being productive, which in her view creates a wave of unhelpful self-criticism. Instead, she claims that any non-related activity that is relevant and comfortable may help procrastinators make progress towards their goal and/or make them feel good while doing it, thus supporting their self-worth and motivating them to continue. After all, getting things done gives you a great sense of achievement: As Joseph Ferrari notes for procrastinators, “they’re missing life. Life is too short. You need to get things done”.

So do the thing – and move on with your life.
